# SIUDEO prototype: conversion research handoff

## Executive conclusion

The strongest direction for SIUDEO is an art-led storefront that makes the work easy to understand and the maker easy to believe. Feature Brianne on the homepage, but do it as **evidence of authorship, process, and artistic intent**: a real studio portrait, factual biography, credentials, and a clear path to her work. The “handmade effect” links handmade identification with product attractiveness through perceived human care, while artist-authenticity research connects perceptions of the artist to evaluations of the work and behavioural intentions ([Journal of Marketing](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jm.14.0018); [Psychology & Marketing](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mar.20719)). Etsy likewise recommends showing people, workspace, and process to build connection and trust ([Etsy Help](https://help.etsy.com/hc/en-us/articles/115015628487-How-to-Edit-Your-Shop-s-About-Section?segment=selling)).

That is not the same as evidence that a generic headshot, by itself, directly increases ecommerce conversion. No such causal evidence was found in this review. Brianne’s section should therefore support the product story, not replace products at the top of the page, and its effect should be measured.

## How the design creates emotion without naming it

Emotional response is treated as an outcome of the visitor’s own imagination, not a claim the interface repeats. Four mechanisms guide the revised copy and art direction:

1. **Mental simulation:** product depictions that make interaction easy to imagine can increase purchase intention ([Elder & Krishna, Journal of Consumer Research](https://doi.org/10.1086/661531)). The hero therefore shows the cups being held, while the copy names coffee, tea, and passing a cup around the table. Future photography should add in-hand scale and table-use views.
2. **Concrete sensory detail:** concrete language can signal attention and understanding, while sensory language can make described product use more credible ([Packard & Berger, Journal of Consumer Research](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucaa038); [Cascio Rizzo et al., Journal of Consumer Research](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucad017)). The revised language names coloured slip, moulds, pours, surfaces, loops, attachments, capacities, and the way one result varies from the next. It avoids abstract adjective stacks.
3. **Recognizable use:** small routines can increase attention, savoring, and perceived value, though the strongest experimental evidence comes from food rather than ceramics ([Vohs et al., Psychological Science](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-af41-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)). References to cupboards, tables, coffee, and tea provide a familiar scene without instructing the visitor what to feel.
4. **Visible authorship:** process, provenance, and the actual maker imply care more credibly than phrases such as “made with love.” The studio portrait, biography, residency history, and descriptions of Brianne’s method provide this evidence.

Accordingly, the prototype no longer uses “feeling,” “joy,” or identity claims as a recurring message. Its emotional layer comes from imagery, material specificity, variation, everyday use, and the human process behind each object.

## Evidence-backed principles and prototype response

### 1. Remove uncertainty from the first product scan

Baymard recommends keeping price visible and reports that missing essential listing information can make people reject relevant products or repeatedly open detail pages ([Baymard: product-list information](https://baymard.com/blog/product-listing-information)). For ceramics, useful category-specific details include volume, care, production method, and expected variation.

**In this prototype:** the early five-product grid exposes every current work with its name, CAD price, volume, a short factual description, and product photography. A hover/detail image supplies a second inspection view. “Add to cart” is present to demonstrate a familiar future purchase path, but is intentionally non-transactional in this prototype. Prices are not hidden behind a click or softened into “contact for price.”

### 2. Show the range before asking visitors to read the story

First-time visitors use homepage content to infer what a shop sells and how broad its catalogue is. Baymard recommends representing meaningful product breadth and notes that some casual browsers of visually driven products begin by scrolling the homepage ([Baymard: catalogue breadth](https://baymard.com/blog/inferring-product-catalog-from-homepage)). Its large-retailer percentage rule should not be copied mechanically to this tiny catalogue; the transferable principle is to show the range early.

**In this prototype:** all five products appear near the top, immediately after the art-led hero and before the longer artist story. This is effectively the whole present catalogue, making the range more legible than a single featured object would. The hero’s literal “Shop the collection” CTA moves directly to it; “Meet Brianne” is secondary.

### 3. Make inspirational imagery shoppable in meaning, even before checkout exists

Expressive lifestyle imagery creates frustration when the depicted object cannot be found. Baymard recommends placing depicted products beside it or linking to the relevant product or curated list ([Baymard: inspirational product images](https://baymard.com/blog/inspirational-product-images-links)).

**In this prototype:** the hero establishes the visual world of the ceramics and immediately pairs it with “Shop the collection.” Product-led imagery resolves into the five named, priced works below instead of becoming a decorative dead end. In production, any future room, table, or studio image that includes a specific sellable piece should link to that exact piece or a clearly labelled set.

### 4. Let photography do the work of physical inspection

Online buyers cannot hold a mug or judge its scale and surface. Baymard recommends at least one in-scale image with a person, known object, or relevant setting; ambiguity can make people misjudge an item ([Baymard: in-scale product images](https://baymard.com/blog/in-scale-product-images)). Etsy similarly treats multiple images as information and recommends showing goods in context ([Etsy: well-crafted listings](https://www.etsy.com/ca/seller-handbook/article/1347574487014)).

**In this prototype:** large art-directed hero photography creates desire, while each product has both a primary and detail image plus an explicit ounce capacity. Before launch, each work should also receive a consistent set: clean full view, hand-held or table-setting scale view, surface/detail view, and—where variation matters—representative examples. The prototype’s hover swap previews this richer gallery model.

### 5. Sell the human making, not manufactured scarcity

The handmade study links production-mode cues to attractiveness through inferred human care; the effect is context dependent and especially relevant to meaningful gifting ([Journal of Marketing](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jm.14.0018)). Artist-authenticity research suggests the artist matters, but through perceived authenticity—not mere facial visibility ([Psychology & Marketing](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mar.20719)).

**In this prototype:** a compact “Meet Brianne” studio section uses a real environmental portrait, a specific biography, location, and verifiable education/residency credentials. The factual trust strip—“Handmade in Vancouver,” microwave/dishwasher safe, Canada Post shipping, and Metro Vancouver pickup—turns broad brand sentiment into concrete answers. There are no invented countdowns, false stock warnings, blanket sustainability claims, or generic luxury language.

### 6. Use social proof only when it is real

A meta-analysis finds that online-review characteristics influence purchase intention, with effects varying by context ([Data and Information Management](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2543925123000323)). This supports collecting real customer evidence, not fictional five-star reviews. Canada’s Competition Bureau warns against “astroturfing” and requires testimonials to be authorized and accurately represented ([Competition Bureau Canada](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/deceptive-marketing-practices-digest-volume-1); [testimonial guidance](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/deceptive-marketing-practices/types-deceptive-marketing-practices/use-tests-or-testimonials)).

**In this prototype:** trust comes from checkable facts, real credentials, direct Instagram/email links, and honest product details. The design deliberately avoids fabricated reviews, press logos, ratings, customer counts, and scarcity. Later, add customer quotes, exhibition/stockist mentions, or press only with source records, permission, dates, and links where appropriate.

### 7. Prefer literal actions and accessible contrast

Generic CTAs can misalign expectations; Baymard’s testing shows that people may interpret “Get Started” as exploration rather than a request for information, and recommends labels that match the destination ([Baymard: CTA expectations](https://baymard.com/blog/consumables-subscriptions-cta)). Its accessibility research also stresses descriptive link labels and visible links ([Baymard: accessible links](https://baymard.com/blog/links-accessibility)). WCAG 2.2 requires at least 4.5:1 contrast for normal text, 3:1 for large text, and 3:1 for meaningful control boundaries and states ([W3C text contrast](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum); [W3C non-text contrast](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast)).

**In this prototype:** “Shop the collection,” “Meet Brianne,” and “Add to cart” state the intended action. The persistent cart count demonstrates future status feedback; email and Instagram links are named. Cobalt, clay, butter, sage, warm paper, and ink create an emotional, studio-like palette, while dark text, solid primary buttons, visible keyboard focus, alt text, a skip link, and reduced-motion support protect usability. The newsletter asks only for an email address with the restrained “Join studio notes” CTA. Colour is a brand hypothesis—not a universal conversion formula.

## Future admin handoff

The admin source files are retained for future exploration, but this prototype does not expose an admin route. Before production, observe Brianne adding a real item and test field language, validation, draft/publish states, and error recovery. A real admin also needs authentication, secure storage, image processing, backups, and accessible validation.

## Caveats and measurement plan

This evidence is directional, not a forecast. Baymard primarily reports usability findings from larger ecommerce sites; the academic handmade and artist-authenticity studies often measure attractiveness, attitudes, or intention rather than completed purchases on an independent ceramics site. The current prototype has no checkout, inventory, or working cart, so its clicks can measure comprehension and intent only. All care, shipping, location, price, credential, and availability claims must be confirmed before launch.

First run moderated mobile and desktop task tests: ask what is sold, what a piece costs, how large it is, who made it, and what each CTA will do. Instrument hero CTA clicks, product/detail engagement, add-to-cart intent, artist-section reach, newsletter intent, and Instagram/email exits; record element exposure so a non-click is interpretable.

When real checkout and sufficient traffic exist, predefine one primary metric—completed purchase rate or revenue per eligible session—and guardrails such as product discovery and cart abandonment. Test one hypothesis at a time:

1. Process-led Brianne section versus a product-only control, keeping catalogue position unchanged.
2. In-scale/use photography versus object-only photography.
3. All five products early versus a smaller featured selection with the remainder one step away.
4. Factual trust strip versus no strip.
5. Once genuine proof exists, sourced customer/exhibition proof versus factual trust content alone.

Use a predetermined test duration/sample requirement and review mobile and desktop for harmful differences. If traffic is too low for a reliable A/B result, prioritize repeated task-based usability sessions, first-click tests, customer interviews, and before/after cohort evidence. The goal is not to make SIUDEO more “salesy”; it is to preserve Brianne’s artistic point of view while reducing the unanswered questions that stop an interested visitor from acting.
